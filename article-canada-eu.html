TRADE

Canada just got an unprecedented offer from the EU

Pierre Sep 16 6 min read

This week, European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen made an unusual proposal during her annual State of the Union address in Strasbourg: she invited Canada to become the EU's first-ever "associate member." Prime Minister Mark Carney was in the audience.

There's just one problem — that status doesn't exist. It isn't defined anywhere in EU treaties. If this goes forward, the EU and Canada would essentially have to invent a new category of partnership from scratch.

Why this isn't EU membership — or the Norway model

Canada can't join the EU outright because it isn't a European state. That's a hard geographic rule, not a political choice. This also isn't the Norway model, where a country adopts most EU regulations and pays into the EU budget in exchange for market access, without getting a vote on the rules it has to follow. Canadian officials have said that's explicitly not what's on the table. What's being described instead is something looser: a much closer alliance than a trade deal, but with no transfer of sovereignty and no obligation to copy-paste EU law.

The relationship already runs deep

This isn't happening in a vacuum. Canada and the EU have been trading under CETA — the Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement — since it was provisionally applied in 2017. It eliminated tariffs on roughly 99% of goods traded between the two economies, and the numbers show it: two-way trade in goods and services has grown by more than 70% since CETA took effect, crossing €130 billion in 2025. The EU is now Canada's second-largest trading partner after the United States, and EU companies have built up close to €245 billion in direct investment in Canada. In other words, the "associate member" idea isn't inventing a relationship from scratch — it's asking how much closer an already substantial one can get.

Why now

Canada is in the middle of a trade war with the United States, its largest and closest trading partner, and one it has historically sent roughly three-quarters of its exports to. That's pushed Ottawa to actively look for other economic and strategic partners it can rely on. The EU, watching the same strain on old alliances play out from its own side of the Atlantic, wants to lock in a partner it sees as stable, resource-rich, and rules-based — at a moment when both sides have an incentive to diversify away from Washington.

We want to bring the relationship with Canada to the highest level possible.

— Ursula von der Leyen, President of the European Commission, State of the Union address, Sept 16, 2026

What it could concretely change

Because the status doesn't exist yet, nothing is locked in — but based on how officials on both sides have described the goal, a few areas keep coming up as likely building blocks:

Trade and investment: going beyond CETA's remaining exceptions (some agricultural products and government procurement still carry restrictions) toward something closer to full market access.

Regulatory cooperation: aligning rules in specific sectors — like clean technology, critical minerals, or digital policy — without Canada adopting the EU's rulebook wholesale.

Security and defence: deeper coordination on things like defence procurement and critical infrastructure, an area where Canada has already signaled interest in reducing reliance on U.S. suppliers.

Mobility: easier movement for workers, students, or businesspeople between Canada and EU member states, though this is the area officials have said the least about so far.

What's still unknown

Nobody — including Canada's own ambassador-designate to the EU — has said which of these pieces would actually make it into a final agreement, or how they'd be legally structured given that "associate member" has no precedent. Carney addressed the European Parliament the following day, which offered an early signal of intent but not a blueprint. Expect this to move in stages over months, not weeks — treaty-like arrangements between Canada and the EU have historically taken years to negotiate and ratify, and this one starts with an extra step nobody has done before: defining the category itself.
